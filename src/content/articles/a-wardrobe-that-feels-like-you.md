The mirror is rarely where the trouble starts. It starts earlier, in a shop or on a website, with a version of yourself you imagine you might become: more polished, more spontaneous, the kind of person who wears silk to breakfast. You buy the garment on behalf of that person. Then it hangs in the wardrobe for two years, tags still on, waiting for a life that has not arrived.

Most of us do not need more clothes. We need a clearer relationship with the ones we own. A wardrobe that feels like you is not a perfect capsule or a colour-coded rail. It is simply a collection in which nearly everything has a reason to be there, and nearly everything gets worn.

## Begin with what you reach for

Before you edit a single hanger, spend two weeks noticing. Keep a note in your phone each morning: what you put on, what the day asks of you, and whether the outfit felt right. Do not aim for a flattering record. Aim for evidence.

At the end of the fortnight, patterns tend to appear. There is the jumper you wear for every difficult meeting. The trousers that require no thought. The dress that only works when you are standing up. Often, a third of the wardrobe does most of the living, while the rest waits for an occasion that never quite announces itself.

Ask three questions of the clothes you reach for:

- What is it about this piece that makes it easy — the fabric, the cut, the colour, the fact that it has pockets?
- When does it feel most like me, and when does it feel like a costume?
- What does it need from me? Ironing, special underwear, a confidence I do not have today?

The answers are more useful than any style rule. They describe a real life, not an aspirational one.

## Fit is a feeling, not a number

Size tags are unreliable and inconsistent. A better measure is movement. When you put something on, sit down. Reach above your head. Cross your arms. Walk to the other end of the room. Does the garment move with you, or do you shrink to accommodate it?

Pay attention to the places where clothes most often betray us: shoulders that pull, waistbands that dig, armholes that cut, waist seams that sit an inch wrong. A tailor can often solve these small frictions for less than the price of a new piece. Taking up a hem or adjusting a shoulder strap can turn an almost-right garment into one you wear weekly.

The goal is not a perfect fit in the abstract. It is a fit for your actual body, on an actual Tuesday, when you are rushing and distracted and need your clothes to be on your side.

## Build a palette you can live in

There is a difference between the colours you admire on other people and the colours that make you feel recognisable. Hold a fabric to your face in daylight, not under the yellow glow of a changing room. Notice whether your face comes forward or retreats. Notice what you pair it with without thinking.

A workable palette is usually less restrictive than it sounds. It might be a handful of neutrals with one saturated coat. It might be denim, cream, and oxblood; or ink, grey, and a green you keep returning to. The point is recurrence. If every new purchase can meet two things you already own, the wardrobe begins to work as a wardrobe rather than a set of separate wishes.

## The pause before you buy

Impulse buys are rarely about the garment. They are about the promise attached to it: a new season, a new job, a version of the week that feels less ordinary. The garment cannot carry that promise alone. It can only be a garment.

A simple pause helps. Add the thing to a list instead of a basket and wait three days. Then ask:

1. What will this replace, or what gap does it fill?
2. Can I name three things I already own that it works with?
3. Would I choose it if it were the only new thing I bought this season?

If the answer to the second question is vague, the garment is probably a duplicate in disguise. If the answer to the third is no, you have saved yourself the small administrative burden of returning it. None of this requires a vow of austerity. It just asks for a little more intention than the checkout button.

## Edit without punishment

The hard part of a wardrobe edit is not deciding what to remove. It is deciding what to do with the tenderness attached to clothes. The dress from a good summer. The jacket bought for an interview you did not get. The jeans that fit once.

Give yourself a holding box instead of a bin bag. Store the uncertain pieces for a season, out of sight. If you do not go looking for them, they have already answered. For everything else, choose the next home with care: mended and reworn, passed to a friend, sold, or donated through an organisation that genuinely accepts what you have. Do not mistake a clear-out for a solution to the volume of clothing in the world. The most sustainable garment is still the one that stays in use.

An honest wardrobe is not necessarily small. It is alive. It holds clothes for your work, your rest, the weather you actually have, and the few occasions that are worth dressing for. It contains things that have been worn enough to belong to you.

The mirror is not the final judge. The day is. Choose what lets you meet it.
